Note-taking systems for research

Academic Writing, Reading Research and Presenting

Read forty papers over a semester without a system, and by paper thirty you'll have forgotten what paper five actually said, or why you thought it mattered in the first place. The problem usually isn't reading too little — it's that notes taken haphazardly are nearly as useless as no notes at all when you actually sit down to write.

What a research note actually needs to capture

Regardless of the specific system you use, a genuinely useful note on a source usually includes more than just "what it says":

  • The core claim or finding, in your own words (not a copied quote — see Avoiding Plagiarism).
  • Why you're noting it — how it relates to your project, or what question it answers for you. This is the piece people skip most often, and the one that makes a note useful months later instead of a mysterious fragment.
  • How it relates to other sources you've read — does it agree, disagree, extend, or contradict something else in your notes?
  • The full citation, ideally linked directly via your reference manager.

The Zettelkasten method

One well-known system worth knowing, developed by sociologist Niklas Luhmann (who used it to produce an extraordinarily prolific body of work over his career): write each idea as its own short, self-contained note, then link related notes to each other rather than filing them under a single rigid category. Over time this builds a genuine network of connected ideas, rather than a pile of isolated summaries — and the connections between notes are often where new insights and paper structures actually come from.

A commonly used version distinguishes a few note types:

  • Fleeting notes — quick, temporary jottings made while reading or thinking, to be processed later.
  • Literature notes — notes tied to a specific source, capturing its key claims in your own words.
  • Permanent notes — a single, refined idea, written so it would make sense on its own even out of context, and linked to related permanent notes.

Tools like Obsidian, Roam Research, and even a well-organised Notion database can support this style of linked note-taking digitally.

Is Zettelkasten overkill for your project?

Possibly, and that's fine. It has real upfront overhead, and it pays off most for long, multi-source projects (a thesis or dissertation spanning months, an extended literature review) where a growing web of connected ideas is genuinely valuable. For a shorter assignment or a smaller-scale project, a simpler system — one structured note per source, organised by theme in your reference manager or a shared document — is often perfectly sufficient, and much less effort to maintain.

Why is this important?

Good notes are what let the synthesis that a strong literature review requires actually happen — you can't group sources by theme, or notice where they disagree, if all you have is a pile of disconnected summaries you barely remember writing. Time spent on genuinely structured notes while reading is time saved, many times over, when you sit down to actually write.

Tips

  • Take notes during (or immediately after) reading, not days later from memory — the specific details fade fast, even for papers that felt memorable at the time.
  • Write notes in your own words as a matter of habit, not just for plagiarism reasons — it also forces you to actually understand a claim well enough to restate it, which is a good check on whether you really grasped it.
  • Whatever system you use, review your notes periodically, not just once while writing the final document — connections between sources are often easier to spot in a second pass than the first time through.
  • Don't let building the "perfect" note-taking system become its own procrastination — a simple, consistently used system beats an elaborate one you abandon after two weeks.
