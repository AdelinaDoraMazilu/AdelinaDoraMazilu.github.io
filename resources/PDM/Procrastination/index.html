Overcoming procrastination

Project Development and Management

You know the assignment is due. You know roughly what needs to happen. And yet, somehow, cleaning your desk, reorganising your files, or watching "just one more video" feels more possible right now than opening the document. If that sounds familiar, you're not lazy, and you're not alone — you're doing something almost every student (and almost every professional) does. Understanding why makes it much easier to actually work with it instead of just feeling guilty about it.

Why is this important?

Procrastination isn't just an efficiency problem — on a team project, your procrastination becomes someone else's blocker. A delayed task doesn't just cost you time; it can stall a teammate whose work depends on yours, and it compresses the whole team's schedule at exactly the point (right before a deadline) when there's the least room left to absorb problems.

Why we actually procrastinate

It's rarely about the task being unimportant. More often, procrastination shows up when a task is vague, feels too big, feels aversive (boring, confusing, or anxiety-inducing), or when the payoff feels far away and abstract compared to the immediate relief of doing something easier instead. Notice that three of those four causes are directly addressed by other resources in this category: breaking work down so it isn't vague or too big, and narrowing scope so the task in front of you is actually concrete. Procrastination is often less a willpower problem and more a sign that a task hasn't been broken down small enough yet.

The Pomodoro Technique

One of the most studied and simplest tools here: work in short, timed bursts (traditionally 25 minutes) followed by a short break (5 minutes), with a longer break after every four bursts. Developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s, the technique works partly because starting a task — even for a short, bounded period — lowers the mental resistance to beginning at all; committing to 25 minutes feels far more manageable than committing to "finish the assignment."

Early research on the technique is still limited, but what exists is encouraging: one small diary study of graduate students found the technique reduced procrastination behaviour in the large majority of participants, and other studies have found more modest but still positive effects on focus and motivation.

Other techniques that help

  • The two-minute rule. If starting genuinely feels impossible, commit to just two minutes of it. Often the hardest part is the first move, not the work itself, and two minutes in you'll frequently find yourself willing to keep going.
  • Make the first step absurdly small and concrete. Not "write the report" but "open a blank document and write one sentence describing what the report is about." Vague tasks invite procrastination; specific, tiny ones don't.
  • Remove friction from starting, and add friction to distractions. Close the tabs that will tempt you before you start, not after you've already gotten distracted. Environment design consistently beats willpower.
  • Body-double. Working alongside someone else, even silently on separate tasks (in person or on a call), makes many people noticeably less likely to drift off task.

When it's more than occasional procrastination

Everyone puts things off sometimes — that's normal and not something to panic about. But if avoidance is constant, is affecting most areas of your work or life, or comes with a lot of anxiety, shame, or exhaustion attached to it, that's worth talking to someone about — a tutor, your university's student support or counselling service, or a doctor — rather than trying to fix with a productivity technique alone. Techniques like Pomodoro help with the everyday version of procrastination; they aren't a substitute for support if something deeper is going on.

Tips

  • Pick a fixed length that works for you — 25 minutes is a starting point, not a rule. Some people focus better with 15-minute or 45-minute blocks.
  • Write down the single next physical action before you start ("open the file and write the intro paragraph"), not the whole task. It's much easier to begin something specific than something abstract.
  • Track your sessions loosely, even just tally marks. Seeing visible progress is motivating in a way that a distant deadline usually isn't.
  • Be honest with your team early if you're stuck or behind, rather than silently hoping to catch up. See Effective Teamwork — a five-minute honest conversation is almost always cheaper than the alternative.
