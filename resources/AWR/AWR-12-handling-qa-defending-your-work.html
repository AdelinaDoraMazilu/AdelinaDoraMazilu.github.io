Handling Q&A and defending your work

Academic Writing, Reading Research and Presenting

For a lot of students, the Q&A session feels more frightening than the prepared talk itself — there's no script, and it feels like anything could be asked. In practice, questions after a presentation or in a viva are far more predictable than they seem in advance, and a few habits make a genuinely large difference to how confident you'll come across.

Most questions fall into a handful of predictable categories

  • Clarification — "Can you explain what you meant by...?" Often genuinely just a request for more detail, not a challenge to your work.
  • Methodology — "Why did you choose this approach over an alternative?" or "How did you control for X?" Preparing justifications for your key design decisions in advance covers most of these.
  • Significance and limitations — "How does this compare to existing work?" or "What are the limitations of your approach?" These connect directly to your literature review and discussion section, so the content is usually already in your head — the question is really asking you to say it out loud.
  • Extension — "What would you do differently, or next?" A chance to show you understand your own work's boundaries, not a trap.

How to answer well

  • Pause before answering. A few seconds of silence to think feels much longer to you than it does to the audience. Taking that time to actually think produces a better answer than rushing into one.
  • Make sure you understood the question. If it's unclear or could mean two different things, briefly restate it or ask for clarification before answering — answering the wrong question confidently is worse than asking for a moment to understand it.
  • Answer directly first, then explain. Give the actual answer in your first sentence, then elaborate. Burying the answer at the end of a long explanation makes it harder for the audience to follow, and can look evasive even when it isn't.
  • It's fine to say "I don't know" or "that's outside what I looked at." Honestly acknowledging a limitation is far stronger, and more credible, than improvising an answer that oversells your work.
  • Stay calm with challenging or skeptical questions. A pointed question about your methodology is usually a sign someone engaged seriously with your work, not necessarily an attack — respond to the substance of the question, not the tone.

Why is this important?

How you handle unscripted questions tells an examiner or audience a great deal about whether you actually understand your own work, beyond what you rehearsed. A talk that goes perfectly but falls apart the moment a real question is asked raises doubts a polished slide deck can't fix — conversely, handling a tough question well can leave a stronger impression than the prepared talk itself.

Preparing in advance

  • Before your talk, write out the three or four questions you're most worried about being asked, and actually draft answers to them — the anticipation is often worse than answering the real question turns out to be.
  • Ask a peer or supervisor to "grill" you with likely questions as a practice run, ideally including at least one genuinely critical one.
  • Know your own work's limitations before anyone else points them out to you — being the first to name a weakness, where relevant, reads as self-aware rather than defensive.
  • Re-read your own document shortly before the talk or viva, specifically looking for any claims you might be asked to justify or defend.

Tips

  • Thank the person for the question before answering, even briefly — it buys you a second to think and keeps the exchange collegial.
  • If you genuinely don't understand a question after asking for clarification once, it's fine to say so honestly rather than guessing at what was meant.
  • Keep answers reasonably concise. A long, rambling answer to a Q&A question is harder to follow than a tight, direct one, even if both cover the same ground.
