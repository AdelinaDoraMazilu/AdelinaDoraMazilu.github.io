Public speaking and presentation delivery

Academic Writing, Reading Research and Presenting

Public speaking anxiety is one of the most commonly reported fears there is — you are very much not alone if the thought of presenting your work makes your palms sweat. The reassuring part: nervousness doesn't have to be eliminated to give a good talk, it just has to be managed well enough not to derail you. Even experienced, confident presenters still get a bit nervous before they go on.

Before the talk

  • Prepare thoroughly, then practice out loud. Knowing your material reduces nerves more reliably than almost anything else — but knowing it silently in your head isn't the same as being able to say it fluently. Rehearse standing up, speaking the words aloud, ideally in front of at least one other person.
  • Practice with your actual slides once they're built (see Designing Effective Slides), not just your notes on their own — the transitions between slides are often where a talk falls apart if unrehearsed.
  • Time yourself, and adjust content rather than rushing on the day if you're running long. It's much better to cut content deliberately in advance than to speed through your conclusion under time pressure.
  • Arrive early to the room or check your setup ahead of time (especially for remote presentations) — last-minute technical scrambling is one of the most avoidable sources of pre-talk stress.
  • Prepare for likely questions in advance — see Handling Q&A and Defending Your Work.

During the talk

  • Slow down. Nervousness reliably speeds people up without them noticing; a deliberately measured pace is both easier for the audience to follow and, physiologically, tends to calm you down as well.
  • Breathe deliberately before you start and during natural pauses — a few slow breaths genuinely reduce physical nervousness (racing heart, shaky voice) by counteracting the adrenaline response.
  • Make eye contact around the room, rather than fixing on one spot, your slides, or your notes the whole time. It helps the talk feel like a conversation rather than a recitation.
  • Pause instead of filling silence with "um" and "err." A brief silent pause while you gather your next thought reads as confident and deliberate; audiences rarely notice it the way you will.
  • Move with purpose, not nervous fidgeting. Some natural movement and hand gestures help engage an audience; repetitive fidgeting (pacing, clicking a pen) tends to distract from it.

Why is this important?

Content and delivery are genuinely separate skills, and a technically strong piece of work can still land poorly if it's delivered in a way that's hard to follow or visibly anxious throughout. Conversely, solid delivery habits can make even a modest result land clearly and confidently. Both are worth deliberately practising, not just the content.

Tips

  • Rehearse enough times that you know your talk's structure cold, but avoid memorising a script word-for-word — a memorised script is fragile (one dropped line can throw the whole thing off) and tends to sound stilted; knowing your key points well enough to explain them naturally is more robust.
  • Build in a consistent pre-talk routine (arrive early, a few deep breaths, a quick run-through of your opening line) — a familiar routine signals to your body that it's time to focus, and reduces decision fatigue right before you start.
  • Practice in front of a smaller, low-stakes audience first (a friend, a study group) before the real presentation, especially if the real audience or stakes feel intimidating.
  • Remember the audience is generally on your side — most people watching a student presentation want you to succeed, not looking for a reason to judge you harshly.
